# Slovene grammar by level (A1–B2), and what Lani teaches

Jan asked which cases belong to which level, and what else would be useful. This page answers from primary
sources: the documents of the Centre for Slovene as a Second and Foreign Language (Center za slovenščino kot drugi
in tuji jezik, Filozofska fakulteta, University of Ljubljana), the Slovene CEFR, the Centre's textbooks and their
teacher's manuals, and research on learners of Slovene. Then it compares that with Lani's grammar book, dialogs
and modules, and ends with recommendations. Sources are at the end, with URLs; [SP] and the like point to them.

## What the sources say, and what they don't

- **There is no official grammar list for A1, A2 or B2 in Slovene.** Only B1 has one: the Threshold Level for
  Slovene (*Sporazumevalni prag za slovenščino*, 2004), whose appendix B is a full grammar "relevant for this level"
  [SP, pp. 185–242; SDTJ, p. 37].
- **A1 has no grammar by design.** The survival level (*Preživetvena raven za slovenščino*, 2016, the A1
  description) says it gives grammar no chapter, because at this level language use is mostly formulaic: set phrases,
  not rules [PR, p. 6]. Its phrases still show which forms an A1 learner meets: *Stara sem devetindvajset let*, *Kako
  ti je ime?*, *Rad bi enoposteljno sobo*, *vroče mi je* [PR, pp. 7–8, 15, 21].
- **The adult programme leaves grammar to the textbooks.** The Centre's programme for adults (*Slovenščina kot drugi
  in tuji jezik*, 2020), which the state exams follow, describes each level by what the learner can do, not by
  grammar, and lists the Centre's textbooks with the CEFR level each leads to [SDTJ, pp. 13–24, 56]. The programme for
  new immigrants (ZIP, 2023, A1 to lower A2) says outright that it defines no grammar and when to teach it, and tells
  teachers to use a textbook that builds grammar "systematically and step by step" [ZIP, pp. 15, 90].
- **The textbook levels are about communication, not grammar.** The Centre linked its textbooks to the CEFR with
  panels who rated the tasks against the CEFR's communicative scales. Grammar drills had no scale to rate against,
  and the authors call the method, without testing, "to a large extent still a matter of inspired guesswork"
  [LFL, pp. 284–287]. So a textbook's order of grammar is its authors' choice, a good one, not a standard.
- **The exams test grammar only in use.** Speaking and writing are marked on "linguistic accuracy" among other
  things; no level from A1 to B2 has a grammar subtest [SDTJ, pp. 28–32]. (The older exams, before 2015, had one at the
  intermediate and advanced levels [LFL, p. 280].)

So a syllabus for Slovene is put together from three things: the CEFR's can-do statements (what a learner must be
able to say at a level, which needs certain forms), the textbooks' order (what the Centre's authors teach when), and
the Threshold's grammar (the ceiling for B1). The textbooks agree on the order and disagree on how much of it is A1.
Where sources disagree, this page says so.

### The CEFR's grammatical accuracy, per level

From the Slovene CEFR [SEJO, p. 138], shortened:

| Level | Accuracy |
|---|---|
| A1 | Controls a few simple structures and sentence patterns from a learnt repertoire. |
| A2 | Uses simple structures correctly, but still makes basic systematic mistakes, e.g. mixes tenses, forgets agreement; what they mean is usually clear. |
| B1 | Fairly accurate in familiar contexts; the first language shows; systematic mistakes, but the meaning is clear. |
| B2 | Good control; slips are rare and often self-corrected; no systematic mistakes that cause misunderstanding. |

This matters for Lani: at A1 and A2 a form is learnt as a phrase first, and systematic mistakes are expected. B2
is where accuracy across the whole system becomes the goal.

### A correction to the overview Jan got

*Star sem 30 let* has no dative: *star* is the adjective in the nominative, agreeing with the speaker (*stara sem*
for a woman), and *let* is the genitive plural that every number from five on takes. The sources teach it exactly
so: *Stara sem devetindvajset let* and the question *Koliko si star?* [PR pp. 7–8], *Star sem 14 let* [ČZS1 1],
*Star sem štirideset let* [ABC 1]. A dative comes in only if the question is put as *Koliko ti je let?* (literally
"how many years are to you"); none of the sources read for this page teaches that form.

## The cases in brief

Jan's first question: which case at which level. Slovene teaching numbers the cases 1 to 6 and gives each a question
with a helper verb [WIKI; OVO]. The levels below are the textbooks' (A1, A2) and the Threshold's (B1); the details
and the sources follow in the syllabus.

| Case | Question | A1 | A2 | B1 and B2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 imenovalnik (nominative) | *kdo? kaj?* (je) | use, with the dual and the plural | | |
| 4 tožilnik (accusative) | *koga? kaj?* (vidim) | use: the object, living masculines (*psa*), *ga, jo*, the plural; *Kam?* with *v, na* (*v šolo*); days (*v soboto*) | *Kam?* with *pod, nad, pred, za, med* | feeling (*zebe me, boli me*), time (*to soboto, dve uri*), *čez, skozi, po* |
| 2 rodilnik (genitive) | *koga? česa?* (ni) | phrases: *iz Kanade*, *30 let*; use after a negation by the end of A1 (*nimam hobija*) | *iz, z, od, do, brez, blizu*, amounts, *koliko*, 5 and more, dates | after *bati se*, the owner (*razstava Klementine Golja*), *zaradi, okoli, sredi …* |
| 5 mestnik (locative) | *o kom? o čem?*, *pri kom? pri čem?* (govorim, sem) | *Kje?* with *v, na, pri*: phrases, the rule by the end of A1 in the adult books; *ob osmih* | *ob, o, po*; the plural (in *Gremo naprej*) | the plural and dual (in *Slovenska beseda v živo 2*) |
| 6 orodnik (instrumental) | *s kom? s čim?* (grem) | phrases: *kruh z maslom*, *z avtobusom* (a rule in *A, B, C*) | *z/s*; *Kje?* with *pod, nad, pred, za, med* | the plural and dual (*z dvema družinama*) |
| 3 dajalnik (dative) | *komu? čemu?* (dam) | phrases: *Kako ti je ime?*, *Všeč mi je.*, *Vroče mi je.* (a rule in *A, B, C*) | *k/h, proti, kljub*; *dati, pomagati, reči komu*; *všeč*; *mu, ji, jim* | feeling (*se mi mudi, se mi zdi*); the plural and dual |

So: at A1 the nominative and the accusative are used, the locative and the genitive after a negation by the end of
A1 in the adult books, and the instrumental and the dative are phrases. At A2 all six are used in the singular, in
the order genitive, locative, instrumental, dative. At B1 come their plurals and duals and the rarer uses [SP pp.
214–218]; B2 adds accuracy, not new cases.

What they agree on:

- **The cases come in one order:** nominative, accusative, then genitive and locative, then instrumental, and the
  dative last. Teachers use this order in practice, although by frequency the accusative and genitive come first
  [NU21, p. 220].
- **Set phrases come before the rule.** *iz Kranja, v Kranju* in the first unit, before any case has a name [ABC];
  *ime mi je, všeč mi je* "as a phrase, to be remembered whole" long before the dative [SBŽ1a manual]; *Bi kavo?* and
  *Jaz bom pivo* without explaining the conditional or the future [SBŽ1a manual].
- **The past and the future are A1.** Every A1 book has both.
- **The cases are learnt in the singular first.** In the *Slovenska beseda v živo* series the beginner learns the
  singular endings of every case, but the dual and plural only of the nominative, genitive and accusative; the
  others' come at B1 [SBŽ2 manual]. *Gremo naprej* has the plural of every case at A2, the dual only in the
  nominative [GN manual].
- **Aspect comes at the end of A2 or at B1**, and is hard: "for learners who don't know a Slavic language, correct
  use is hard to demand" [SBŽ2 manual].

Where they differ: **how much of it is A1.** The adult *A, B, C* has all six cases in the singular by the end of A1;
*Slovenska beseda v živo 1a* has four (nominative, accusative, locative, the genitive after a negation); the
teenagers' book two. The imperative is A1 in *A, B, C*, but in the *Slovenska beseda v živo* series it is "only for
recognition" at the beginner level, to be used at B1 [SBŽ2 manual]. *Gremo naprej* is A2 in the programme and B1 in
its own manual. No A1–B1 book teaches the passive or participles; the Threshold describes them at B1.

## The syllabus, level by level

How to read the tables. **Phrase:** met and used as a set phrase, without the rule (*Kako ti je ime?* long before
the dative). **Use:** the learner makes the form with new words. **Know:** understands it when reading or listening.
A level includes everything below it. The examples are the sources' where they have one, else ours. A number after
a book is its unit.

### B1

B1 has a full grammar of its own: appendix B of the Threshold Level [SP, pp. 185–242]. It describes what a B1 user
needs, not all of which they must produce. Its chapter on tolerance makes getting the message across the main
criterion at B1; whether mistakes in the cases or in aspect that don't block understanding are acceptable, it leaves
to the learner's own ambition, and it recommends aiming a little higher [SP, pp. 175–177].

### B2

No document lists Slovene grammar for B2. The adult programme describes B2 by what the learner does: argues, writes
essays and reports, links a text with "a basic range of cohesive devices", "sometimes" uses "syntactically complex
sentences" and "makes no mistakes that cause misunderstanding" [SDTJ, pp. 16–17, 23]. The Centre's book for B2 to C1
is *Slovenska beseda v živo 3a* [SDTJ, p. 56]. So B2 is the B1 grammar used accurately, plus the grammar of
argument and writing:

## What learners find hard

A caveat first: nearly all the research on errors in Slovene as a second language is on speakers of Serbian,
Croatian, Bosnian and Macedonian, who make up most of the learner corpora [KOST25; JIS22]. For English and German
speakers there is almost nothing beyond teachers' notes in the manuals. So the list below says what is hard for
learners in general, and marks what is known about Jan's languages.

| What | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Word order, above all the short words.** The largest single kind of error in the learner corpus KOST (8,297), ahead of the noun's endings (5,885), the verb's (3,188) and the adjective's (2,512). *uresničile so mi se* for *so se mi*. | KOST25 |
| **The endings of the cases.** Hard for everyone; hardest the locative plural and the dative without a preposition (*širnemu svetu*), which learners replaced with the locative. For speakers of languages without cases, the idea of a case is unclear at first. | JIS22; NU21 |
| **Aspect.** "Demanding especially for learners who don't know a Slavic language; correct use is hard to demand of them." | SBŽ2 manual |
| **The genitive after a negation.** Taught late on purpose (*pijem kavo* → *ne pijem kave*). One exception to know: when the negation is about the object and a contrast follows, the accusative is possible: *Ne smeš brati knjigo, ampak le revijo.* | SBŽ1a manual; ZRC16 |
| **Numbers from five.** Avoided at A1 in *Slovenska beseda v živo 1a*: "*51 v Sloveniji*, not *51 Šolskih ulic v Sloveniji*"; at A2 built on the phrases learners know: *pet evrov, pet minut, pet let*. | SBŽ1a manual; SBŽ1b manual |
| **The imperative.** In the *Slovenska beseda v živo* series only to recognise at the beginner level (*delaš* against *delaj*), to use at B1. | SBŽ2 manual |
| **Reported speech.** Learners shift the tense as their own language does: *Tanja je rekla, da je bila vesela* for *da je vesela*. English does this; German reported speech works differently again. | SBŽ2 manual |
| **Possessives and *svoj*.** Slovene uses *moj, tvoj …* less than English: *Telefoniral bom prijateljici* already means my friend, *To je moja prijateljica* is emphatic. And learners often use *moj* where *svoj* is needed. | SBŽ2 manual |
| **German speakers: *s, z, š, ž*.** "German speakers make mistakes in the pronunciation and spelling of *s, z, š, ž*." Jan's *Gorize* for *Gorica* is this. | SBŽ1a manual |
| **Spoken against written.** The manuals accept spoken forms beside the standard (*bojo*, "more colloquial", beside *bodo*). In speech and the dialects some dual forms become the plural's, but the dual persists. | SBŽ1b manual; JAKOP20 |

Jan's own mistakes so far fit the list: agreement, the verb's ending, where *se* goes, the genitive after *iz*, the
spelling of *c* (see "What Jan gets wrong" below).

## What helps

- **Phrases first, the rule later.** The A1 description leaves grammar out on purpose [PR p. 6]. The A1 manual
  doesn't explain at first that *iz Slovenije* is a genitive, and doesn't correct learners who get it wrong; *ime mi
  je, všeč mi je* are phrases "to be remembered whole"; *Bi kavo?* means *Želite kavo?*, "but we don't explain the
  conditional" [SBŽ1a manual]. The Centre's online course teaches phrases in its first two parts and grammar in the
  third [SLO]. Research agrees: classroom beginners
  used whole phrases unanalysed and, over two years, broke them into parts that fed their own grammar [MHM98].
- **Number the cases, then use the numbers.** It is easier to teach the idea of a case once and then name them
  1–6 (or by their international names) than to explain "here *-a*, there *-o*" every time [SBŽ1a manual]. The
  Centre's tables do the same [OVO; S1+].
- **The case question: useful as a label, not as a method. The sources disagree here.** Slovene schools teach
  each case with a question and a helper verb (*koga ali česa ni?*) [WIKI], and the textbooks pair question, case
  and example: *Kaj je to?* (1), *Kam greš?* (4), *Kje si?* (5) [SBŽ1a manual]. But a study of case teaching,
  following Croatian research, finds deriving the case from the question "not suitable" for learners of Slovene:
  without a native speaker's feel, they can't answer the question in the right case anyway [NU21, pp. 218–220].
  What they propose instead: the case's meaning in a context (*Kam?* after a verb of motion, *Kje?* after a verb
  of being: *Grem v Maribor* / *Živim v Mariboru*), then its forms [NU21, p. 224]. So the question should come with
  its trigger and a model answer, never alone.
- **Aspect has its own questions.** *Kaj delam? Kaj se dogaja?* for the imperfective, *Kaj naredim? Kaj se
  zgodi?* for the perfective [ČZS2 p. 72]; the B1 manual uses the pair *Kaj dela direktor? / Kaj je naredil
  direktor?* with pictures, and signal words (*medtem ko* takes the imperfective) [SBŽ2 manual].
- **Endings are good cues.** English speakers learnt Russian case endings earlier than German ones, although
  Russian has more of them, because a Russian ending tells the case more reliably [KM98]. Slovene is like Russian
  here (our reading): the ending is on the noun. Lots of input with the ending in view pays.
- **Colour and bold help a little, with a task.** The manuals colour question words, endings and prepositions, shade
  in orange the forms to master first, and print in grey the forms only to recognise [SBŽ2 manual; SBŽ1a manual].
  Highlighting alone helps only slightly (d = 0.22 over 16 studies) [LH08]. Lani's bold differences in the choices
  are right, and work with the why and a question, not instead of them.
- **Cards for the short words.** Learners write a sentence's words on cards and look for where the short words go
  [SBŽ2 manual]. In an app: tiles to drag into order.
- **Prompt, don't only recast.** Feedback that makes the learner produce the form works better than hearing the
  right form [LS10]. Lani's typed and said turns are prompts; the hint chip should be one too: a question, not the
  answer.

Slovene teacher would:

page, and let a turn override it. For Jan, add the German question where the logic is German's: *Kje?* is *Wo?*,
*Kam?* is *Wohin?* (the same idea as the German two-way prepositions, other cases), *pomagati* takes the dative like
*helfen*.

### The long press on a line

The word lookup already gives a form's grammar ("locative singular"). The long press adds why, word by word, in one
short line each:

- the **trigger**: *v* + *Kje?* → mestnik; *pomagati* → dajalnik; *pet* → rodilnik množine; *ni* → rodilnik;
- the **question** the word answers (*Kje?*, *Komu?*);
- for a verb: the person, the tense and, once aspect has a page, the pair (*napisal*, perfective; *pisal*);
- the **short words** of the line marked with their slot (*sem – se – mi – ga – je*), when there are two or more;
- a link to the page, as the why has.

The triggers are one small table (the prepositions and their cases, the verbs with a dative or genitive, the
numbers, the negation). The case of each word is the lookup's; where a preposition takes two cases (*v, na, pod,
za …*), the form tells which. For the curated lines this can be worked out once, offline, and checked by hand: the
Slovene pipeline CLASSLA-Stanza gives each word its lemma, case, number, gender and, for verbs, aspect, and links a
noun to its preposition or verb [CLASSLA].
